## Executive Summary

Because the user left the topic unspecified and asked that the attached PDF define the primary focus, the central topic of this report is Terence Irwin's article "Vice and Reason," attached as the PDF and published in *The Journal of Ethics* 5(1) (2001): 73–97. The attached text file also makes clear that the aim is not a bare summary, but a guided map of the argument, its background, and its scholarly setting.

Irwin's core problem is an apparent inconsistency in Aristotle: vicious people seem to act "on decision" or *prohairesis*, which suggests rational guidance, yet Aristotle also says that they live according to passion, not according to reason. Irwin's solution is that the vicious person does use reason, but uses it in a subordinate, instrumental way: practical reason serves antecedent inclinations ⟦corrupt: "ra ally assessing" → likely "rather than rationally assessing"⟧ and ordering them in light of the *kalon* ("the fine" or "the noble"). On this reading, vice is not mere impulsiveness or weakness of will; it is a stable condition in which reason is active but normatively compromised. (Irwin, "Vice and Reason," *The Journal of Ethics*, 2001).

The article matters because it links several hard Aristotelian topics into one interpretive package: the function argument, the division of soul, *prohairesis*, *boulēsis*, practical wisdom, the *kalon*, pleasure, regret, and self-love. Irwin argues that the virtuous person differs from the vicious person not merely by having "better desires," but by treating reason as authoritative over ends, not just as a calculator of means. That makes the paper a major intervention in Aristotelian moral psychology, and later scholarship has continued to debate exactly this point. (Aristotle's Ethics, SEP, 2022; Aristotle's Psychology, SEP, 2004; Brickhouse, "Does Aristotle Have a Consistent Account of Vice?", 2003; ⟦corrupt: "M," 2015" → likely "Müller, 'Aristotle on Vice,' 2015"⟧; Nielsen, "Vice in the Nicomachean Ethics," 2017; Barney, "Becoming Bad," 2020; Solis, "Curable and Incurable Vice in Aristotle," 2025).

My overall assessment is that Irwin's paper remains highly valuable for three reasons. First, it shows why Aristotle cannot be read as treating vice as simply *akrasia* or loss of control. Second, it clarifies the role of the *kalon* in distinguishing virtuous from vicious agency. Third, it frames a debate that later scholars still inhabit, even when they reject his conclusions. Its main weakness is one Irwin himself partly acknowledges: the account fits some vices, such as cowardice and intemperance, better than others, such as greed, vindictiveness, and domination. (Irwin, 2001; Roochnik, "Aristotle's Account of the Vicious," 2007; Nielsen, *Vice in Ancient Philosophy*, 2023/2024).

## Detailed Findings

For a reader with no philosophy background, the paper is about a familiar human problem: ⟦corrupt: "can a bad persor than merely impulsive?" → likely "can a bad person be rational rather than merely impulsive?"⟧ Irwin says Aristotle's answer is yes, but only in a limited sense. A vicious person can plan, deliberate, and choose coherently. What makes that person bad is that reason is not being used to ask, "What is genuinely worth doing?" but only, "How do I get what I already want?" On Irwin's interpretation, virtue requires more than self-control; it requires a rational appreciation of what is fine or worthwhile in itself. (Irwin, 2001; Aristotle's Ethics, SEP, 2022).

For an undergraduate philosophy reader, the paper can be summarized more precisely this way: Irwin reconstructs Aristotle's account of vice as a condition in which *prohairesis* remains operative, so vice cannot be equated with *akrasia*; however, the vicious person's deliberation is governed by a conception of practical reason that treats ends as fixed by inclination and treats reason as merely strategic. The virtuous person, by contrast, chooses actions ⟦corrupt: "for the  means that reason is authoritative" → likely "for the sake of the fine, which means that reason is authoritative"⟧ not only in selecting means but also in identifying and endorsing ends. This difference helps explain Aristotle's claims about pleasure, regret, self-love, and the instability of vice. (Irwin, 2001; Aristotle's Psychology, SEP, 2004; Ancient Theories of Freedom and Determinism, SEP, 2020).

Irwin's central argument can be reconstructed as follows. First, Aristotle distinguishes vice from *akrasia* because the vicious person acts on decision, while the akratic acts against decision. Second, if vice involves decision, the vicious person must be guided by some exercise of reason. Third, Aristotle also says that vicious people live according to passion rather than according to reason. Fourth, these claims can be reconciled if vice is a condition in which reason remains active but is restricted to instrumental service of antecedent inclination. Fifth, the virtuous person differs by choosing actions for the sake of the *kalon*, ⟦corrupt: "notr what appears advantageous" → likely "not merely for what appears advantageous"⟧ or pleasurable. Sixth, because the vicious person lacks this non-instrumental rational commitment, vice is unstable and peculiarly vulnerable to regret and defective self-love. This is Irwin's interpretation of Aristotle, though he also admits that he is "develop[ing] some of his remarks beyond anything he says." (Irwin, 2001).

Several key claims come directly from the attached PDF. Irwin explicitly states the initial puzzle in the article's abstract; he argues that Aristotle's treatment of vice must be read through the pursuit of the fine and the expedient, the connection between vice and pleasure, and the special regret of the vicious person; he says vice is distinct from incontinence because the vicious act on decision; and he concedes near the middle of the paper that he is pushing Aristotle's remarks further than Aristotle explicitly does. He also closes by saying that his account works best for some vices and less ⟦corrupt: "less wendictiveness" → likely "less well for greed, vindictiveness"⟧, and domination. Those points are all in the attached article itself.

A major strength of the paper is that it unifies scattered Aristotelian passages from *Nicomachean Ethics* I, II, III, VII, and IX rather than treating Book VII on vice and *akrasia* and Book IX on self-love and regret as unrelated. A major interpretive risk is that the unifying mechanism — the idea that vicious reason is strategic but not authoritative over ends — depends heavily on Irwin's reading of the *kalon* and on his stronger distinction between "acting for the sake of the fine" and merely pursuing what appears advantageous. That is philosophically powerful, but it is also the point at which later scholars most often resist him. (Irwin, 2001; Cooper, "Reason, Moral Virtue, and Moral Value," 1996; Lear, "Aristotle on Moral Virtue and the Fine," 2006; Moss, "'Virtue Makes the Goal Right'," 2011).

## Aristotelian Background and Evidence Synthesis

⟦corrupt: "Irwin's paper  of Aristotle." → likely "Irwin's paper presupposes a great deal of Aristotle."⟧ The most basic background is the function argument in *Nicomachean Ethics* I.7, where Aristotle identifies the human good with excellent rational activity, and I.13, where he distinguishes the part of the soul that has reason from the part that can listen to reason. This matters because moral virtue belongs to the non-rational but reason-responsive side of the soul, whereas practical wisdom belongs to the reasoning side. Irwin uses exactly this architecture to show why vice cannot simply be "absence of virtue": Aristotle's own psychology seems to demand some positive account of how vice relates to reason. (Aristotle's Ethics, SEP, 2022; Ancient Ethical Theory, SEP, 2023; Aristotle's Psychology, SEP, 2004).

The next indispensable background is *Nicomachean Ethics* II and III. Aristotle defines virtue as a state "concerned with choice" and as a mean relative to us, determined by reason; he then analyzes voluntariness, deliberation, wish, and decision. Irwin leans heavily on those ⟦corrupt: "those book must still involve prohairesis" → likely "those books to argue that vice must still involve prohairesis"⟧ if it is to remain voluntary and blameworthy in Aristotle's sense. That lets him distinguish vice from *akrasia*: the akratic knows the right thing and fails to do it under passion, whereas the vicious person's decision itself is corrupted. (Aristotle's Ethics, SEP, 2022; Ancient Theories of Freedom and Determinism, SEP, 2020; Moral Character, SEP, 2019).

Irwin's reading of the *kalon* is equally central. He treats acting "for the sake of the fine" as choosing an action because of its non-instrumental worth, not because it satisfies a prior inclination. That is why he contrasts the virtuous person's orientation to the fine with the vicious person's orientation to the expedient or advantageous. This line is consistent with later work by Irwin on *kalon* and with broader scholarship that treats Aristotle's virtuous agent as motivated by something irreducible to plain utility or pleasure. (Irwin, 2001; ⟦corrupt: "Irwin, 'The Sensein Aristotle,' 2010" → likely "Irwin, 'The Sense and Reference of Kalon in Aristotle,' 2010"⟧; Cooper, 1996; Lear, 2006).

The paper also sits in a broadly Platonic background. Plato's *Republic* provides the best-known ancient model of psychic division and harmony, with reason, spirit, and appetite ordered into justice; Glaucon, Thrasymachus, and the tyrannical soul supply the challenge of whether injustice can be rationally organized; and the *Gorgias* provides Callicles' immoralist defense of pleasure, superiority, and anti-conventional justice. Irwin explicitly invokes Callicles and Thrasymachus as figures who appear to choose vice on principle, only to deny that Aristotle treats ordinary vicious agents that way. His contrast between concern for the fine and strategic concern for advantage also recalls the tension between reason and desire in the *Phaedrus*. (Callicles and Thrasymachus, SEP, 2025; Plato's Ethics and Politics in the Republic, SEP, 2019; ⟦corrupt: "Ancient Ethical Theory, SEP, 2004, OUP, 1995" → likely two merged entries: "Ancient Ethical Theory, SEP, 2004" and "Irwin, Plato's Ethics, OUP, 1995"⟧).

Irwin's brief appeal to Aquinas is methodologically important even though Aquinas is not his focus. In note 1, Irwin says that some of his exposition of Aristotle is adapted from Aquinas, especially Aquinas's distinction among sin from passion, ignorance, and deliberate badness. That matters because Irwin is effectively asking whether Aristotle has a workable analogue to "deliberate badness": wrongdoing that is not merely impulsive and not merely ignorant in the simple sense, but stably, knowingly disordered.

## Scholarly Debate and Reception

Before Irwin, the debate was often framed as a choice between inconsistency and silence. Julia Annas's 1977 article is one of the classic sources Irwin names as charging Aristotle with instability on friendship, self-love, and the psychology of vice, while Jean Hampton's "The Nature of Immorality" is cited by Irwin as a forceful statement that Aristotle vacillates between ignorance and indifference in his account of vice. Sarah Broadie is the ⟦corrupt: "the thetic predecessor" → likely "the most sympathetic predecessor"⟧ Irwin cites, especially on self-love and friendship. (Irwin, 2001; Annas, "Plato and Aristotle on Friendship and Altruism," *Mind*, 1977; Hampton, "The Nature of Immorality," *Social Philosophy and Policy*, 1990/2009; Broadie, *Ethics with Aristotle*, 1991).

Irwin's immediate post-2001 reception shows both uptake and resistance. Thomas Brickhouse's 2003 article argues that Aristotle can indeed be given a consistent account of vice, and his abstract describes vice as reason-guided pursuit of bodily pleasure under a false view of the good, which is close to the family of views Irwin was trying to stabilize. David Roochnik, by contrast, explicitly treats Aristotle's account of the vicious as a "forgivable inconsistency," which is a direct refusal of Irwin's harmonizing ambition. (Brickhouse, *Review of Metaphysics*, 2003; ⟦corrupt: "Roochnik, ... His)" → likely "Roochnik, History of Philosophy Quarterly, 2007"⟧).

The strongest later challenge to Irwin comes from Jozef Müller. Müller's 2015 abstract rejects what he calls the "widely held view" that Aristotle's vicious agent is a principled follower of a wrong conception of the good whose soul is harmonious like the virtuous person's soul; instead, Müller argues that vice lacks real principles and is even more disharmonious than *akrasia*. Jay Elliott's 2016 "Reply to Müller" then explicitly maps the debate by naming Irwin and Brickhouse as views according to which Aristotle's inconsistency reflects a kind of inconsistency in the vicious person himself. This is clear evidence that Irwin's paper became a reference point in the later dialectic, even when scholars rejected it. (Müller, BJHP, 2015; Elliott, BJHP, 2016).

Karen Margrethe Nielsen's 2017 article and her 2023/2024 Cambridge Element push the debate in another direction. Nielsen argues that Aristotle has a ⟦corrupt: "has a g in the decision" → likely "has a systematic account of 'ignorance in the decision'"⟧, and her Element explicitly returns to the same hard questions Irwin foregrounded: why the vicious agent regrets, how vice relates to moral ignorance, and why vice is indebted to Plato while still confounding readers. Rachel Barney's 2020 chapter likewise treats badness as involving "corrupted reason" and motivated reasoning, while Eric Solis's 2025 article proposes a distinction between curable and incurable vice in order to reconcile the same VII/IX tension over regret and psychic state. These later works do not all accept Irwin's solution, but they plainly inhabit the problem-space he helped define. (Nielsen, *Phronesis*, 2017; Nielsen, *Vice in Ancient Philosophy*, 2023/2024; Barney, "Becoming Bad," 2020; Solis, 2025).

On the substance of the debate, the fault line now looks roughly like this. One side, close to Irwin, says vice still involves decision and therefore some rational ⟦corrupt: "endorsemenly strategically ordered" → likely "endorsement, even if only strategically ordered"⟧ to appetite or apparent good. Another side, closer to Müller and some non-intellectualist readings, says vice is less principled and less harmonious than Irwin allowed. A third line, represented by Nielsen, Barney, Marechal, and some work by Moss, stresses ignorance, appearance, conviction, habituation, and corruption of evaluative outlook rather than strategic reason alone. The debate is therefore no longer "coherent or incoherent?" but "what kind of psychic corruption does Aristotle mean by vice?" (Moss, 2011; Marechal, "Practical Wisdom as Conviction," 2024; Müller, 2015; Nielsen, 2017; Barney, 2020).

## Actionable Conclusions

If the practical aim is to understand Irwin's paper efficiently and well, the best reading sequence is this. First, read *Nicomachean Ethics* I.7 and I.13 for the function argument and the soul's relation to reason; II.1–6 for habituation and the mean; III.1–5 for voluntariness, deliberation, wish, ⟦corrupt: "wish, a, continence, incontinence, and pleasure" → likely "wish, and decision; VII.1–10 for vice, continence, incontinence, and pleasure"⟧; and IX.4 and IX.8 for self-love, regret, and psychic conflict. Then read Irwin's article. After that, read Brickhouse 2003, Müller 2015, Nielsen 2017, Barney 2020, and Solis 2025 to see the principal live options.

For editions and companions, Terence Irwin's Hackett translation is especially useful for this topic because it includes introduction, notes, glossary, and related Aristotelian texts; Broadie and Rowe's Oxford edition remains one of the most philosophically influential commentaries; Roger Crisp's Cambridge translation is compact and readable; and Reeve's Hackett translation is strong for readers who want dense internal cross-referencing across the corpus. For broader orientation, Hardie's *Aristotle's Ethical Theory*, Kraut's *Blackwell Guide to Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics*, and ⟦corrupt: "Jon Miller's As: A Critical Guide" → likely "Jon Miller's Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics: A Critical Guide"⟧ are still among the most useful starting points.

My final substantive conclusion is that Irwin's solution works best when Aristotle's vicious person is taken to be someone whose reason has not disappeared, but whose conception of the role of reason has been morally deformed. Instrumental rationality is therefore insufficient for virtue. What virtue adds is not just correct means-end order, but a rationally educated attachment to ends as fine in themselves. That is why, for Irwin, the vicious person can be deliberative and yet still "live according to passion." Whether Aristotle himself fully and consistently says all of this is contestable; whether Irwin identifies the deepest interpretive problem is much less contestable. (Irwin, 2001; Cooper, 1996; Lear, 2006; ⟦corrupt: "Niels4" → likely "Nielsen, 2023/2024"⟧).
